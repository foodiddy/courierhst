  Disclaimer: this is a HISTORY file. There are no plans in here, no parts
  lists, and nothing that would work today. The thing it describes was
  retired before most of you had your first modem. Read, enjoy, move on.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

  WHAT IT WAS

  In the old long-distance network the machines that connected your call
  talked to each other over the very same wire that carried your voice.
  Instructions like "this trunk is free" or "hang up now" were sent as
  musical beeps, in the open, mixed in with the conversation.

  That is called IN-BAND signalling. It was cheap, it was clever, and it
  had one enormous flaw: anyone who could make the right noise at the
  right moment could talk to the switch as if they were the switch.

  A "blue box" was a gadget that made those noises. That is all I am going
  to say about how. Anyone who tells you more is selling something, or is
  lying, or is a very bored teenager in 1987.

  WHY IT WORKED

  The people who designed the system trusted every user to be a customer.
  Nobody imagined a kid on the other end of the line playing the network
  like a piano. The trust was built into the wires.

  WHY IT DIED

  The phone company moved the control messages OFF the voice path and onto
  a separate data channel that customers never touch. Different names
  over different years: first CCIS, later the big system called SS7.

  Out-of-band signalling means the beeps in your ear are only beeps in
  your ear. The switch is not listening to you any more. That was the end.
  By the early 1990s the old trunks were mostly gone and the boxes were
  museum pieces.

  WHAT WE KEPT

  Hackers learned the real lesson: never put the controls where the
  customers can reach them. The lesson was so good that the phone company
  and every software house had to learn it too.
