  every board has a "history" file. most of them are wrong. this one is
  only mostly wrong. corrections go to me, mail on any board that will still
  have me.

  [ 1. THE WHISTLERS ]

  Before there were modems there were kids with very good ears. A boy named
  Joe Engressia, blind from birth, found he could whistle at a phone line
  and the phone company's equipment would answer him. He later took the
  name Joybubbles and spent his life being cheerful at everybody. The
  world needs more of that.

  Around the same time a toy whistle packed in a box of Cap'n Crunch cereal
  became a legend. The man who made it famous, John Draper, picked up the
  handle "Captain Crunch" and never really got rid of it. Breakfast food
  has a lot to answer for.

  [ 2. THE ARTICLE ]

  In October 1971 Esquire ran "Secrets of the Little Blue Box" by Ron
  Rosenbaum. Overnight every teenager in America wanted to be a phreak, and
  every phone company lawyer wanted a word with them. It is the best advert
  the hobby ever got and the worst thing that ever happened to it.

  Around then two Californians named Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs sold
  little blue boxes in dorm rooms. Yes, THOSE two. Then they went off and
  made computers instead. Moral: the hobby is a fine place to start.

  [ 3. THE NEWSLETTERS ]

  1971   Youth International Party Line starts. Later it becomes TAP, the
         Technological American Party, and then the Technical Assistance
         Program. Photocopied, stapled, mailed in brown envelopes.
  1984   2600 magazine begins. Quarterly, thick, and still printed on paper
         while everybody else went to disk.
  1985   Phrack, the electronic magazine, goes up on a board in Missouri.
         Free to read, free to pass around, which was the whole idea.

  [ 4. THE CRACKDOWN ]

  By 1990 the law was tired of being laughed at. In May that year the
  Secret Service ran Operation Sundevil across many cities. Computers were
  carried out of bedrooms in cardboard boxes. Plenty of parents learned a
  new word that week and it was "modem".

  Around the same time came the E911 document case. A text file about the
  phone company's emergency system moved from board to board and landed in
  Phrack. The publisher, a student called Knight Lightning, was charged. The
  case fell apart when it turned out the "secret" document was sold to the
  public for a few dollars. Everybody learned something. The phone company
  learned to check the price list.

  [ 5. THE POINT ]

  Phreaking was never really about free calls. It was about curiosity: a
  giant machine strung across a continent, and someone asking "what does
  this do?" The calls were just the receipt.

  If you want to read the old files, read them as history. The network they
  talk about has been gone for years and nobody is coming back for it.
