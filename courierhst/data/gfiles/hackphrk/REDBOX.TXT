  This is a nostalgia file. If you want a how-to, you are in the wrong
  decade. Nothing here would work on any phone you will meet.

  --- THE OLD PAYPHONE -----------------------------------------------------

  Once upon a time the payphone on the corner was a very trusting machine.
  When you dropped a coin in the slot, the phone did not decide on its own
  whether you paid. It simply made a little ringing noise down the line, and
  the distant telephone exchange listened and counted.

  So the exchange believed the sounds. It never saw the coins. The system
  was only as honest as the noises it was told about.

  A "red box" was a gadget that told that sort of tale. A tape recorder,
  a pocket gadget, a modified toy: everyone had a story. Nobody I know
  has ever made one work for long, and I know a LOT of liars.

  --- THE LONG GOODBYE -----------------------------------------------------

  The phone company did what any sensible store would do. It changed the
  deal:

   * Phones were moved to "coin first" service. The phone itself now
     decided whether you paid, and told the exchange nothing it could be
     fooled with.
   * Smarter payphones were fitted, and the exchange stopped believing
     any noise that came down the line.
   * Coin sounds got filtered out of the voice path altogether.
   * Some phones lost the coin slot completely and took cards.

  By the mid-90s the red box was a tall tale told by people who were not
  there. The payphones themselves went next, thanks to the pocket phone.

  --- THE LAW ---------------------------------------------------------------

  Theft of service is theft of service. Plenty of kids got a stern lecture,
  a few got a fine, and some got a visit with their parents in the room.
  Not one of them got rich. There were cheaper hobbies, like collecting
  stamps. Or, you know, a roll of quarters.

  --- MORAL ----------------------------------------------------------------

  The best payphone story I know is about a kid who spent a whole summer
  and forty dollars worth of parts trying to save twenty-five cents.
